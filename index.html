How it works

I start choosing a power supply for a Direct Drive FFB wheel by defining what I actually expect from the motor. Maximum torque alone is not enough: I also need to know the speed at which the motor should maintain that torque.

My calculator follows this sequence:

  1. Define the torque and speed requirements.
  2. Calculate the current needed for the required torques.
  3. Find the minimum supply voltage that allows those torques at the specified speeds.
  4. Select a supply voltage and check whether the motor meets the requirements.
  5. Calculate mechanical power, electrical losses and required supply power.
  6. Add a design margin and determine the recommended supply current rating.
The order matters. A high-power supply with insufficient voltage will still not maintain the required torque at high speed.

1. Define the wheel requirements

I use two operating points for the worked example.

The first point requires 10 Nm at 200 rpm. The second requires at least 2 Nm at 300 rpm. I assume this minimum torque is sufficient to rotate the wheel with its rim and attached button box.

I do not require the full 10 Nm at 300 rpm. Maximum torque and maximum speed do not need to occur simultaneously.

This does not mean the controller automatically limits torque to 2 Nm above 200 rpm. The two points define minimum design requirements, not the complete torque characteristic.

4. Account for winding resistance and its temperature rise

Winding resistance is one of the most important supply-sizing parameters. It directly affects both heat produced in the motor and the voltage needed to maintain the required current.

4.1. Why does resistance matter so much?

Motor windings are made from copper wire. Copper is a good conductor, but it is not perfect: it has electrical resistance. When current flows through a winding, some electrical energy becomes heat.

The Joule–Lenz relationship describes this:

P = I²R
P
Power (heat dissipation or work per unit time, as stated in this equation) [W]
I
Current through the specified conductor (RMS for sinusoidal heating calculations) [A]
R
Resistance of the specified conductor [Ω]

P is heat dissipation in W, I is conductor current in A, and R is resistance in Ω. The key point is that losses increase with the square of current. Doubling current quadruples winding losses if resistance stays constant.

For example, for a conductor resistance of 0.175 Ω:

At 10 A: P = 10² · 0.175 = 17.5 W
At 20 A: P = 20² · 0.175 = 70 W
P
Power (heat dissipation or work per unit time, as stated in this equation) [W]

These are example losses in one winding at the stated RMS current. I calculate total three-phase losses later, accounting for all three windings. This already shows why increasing motor torque can increase heat generation so quickly.

4.2. Resistance also affects the required voltage

There is a second, equally important effect. Maintaining current through a winding requires voltage. The steady-state resistive voltage drop is:

UR = IR
UR
Resistive voltage-drop term in the stated voltage convention [V]

Higher current or resistance means a higher voltage drop. In a BLDC motor, the controller therefore needs enough voltage to produce the current required for torque. If voltage is insufficient, it cannot maintain the commanded current and available torque falls.

This is especially important at high speeds, when the rotating motor generates additional voltage. Section 6 explains that effect.

4.3. Why does resistance increase with temperature?

In metals such as copper, electrical resistance increases with temperature. In simplified terms, stronger lattice vibrations in heated copper make electron flow more difficult. A hot winding therefore has more resistance than the same winding at room temperature.

Over the temperature range relevant to my project, I use a linear approximation:

Rhot = Rcold · (1 + α · ΔT)

4.5. How does the resistance increase affect performance?

It causes two unfavorable effects at once.

First, copper losses increase. At the same current, the motor produces more heat. A 19.65% resistance increase also raises copper losses by 19.65% at unchanged current, adding to the thermal load on the windings.

Second, the required voltage increases. To maintain the same current, the controller must overcome a larger winding voltage drop. At low speeds, ample voltage headroom may make this unimportant. At high speeds, a motor already near the voltage limit may lose the ability to maintain current as resistance increases. Available torque, and therefore achievable mechanical power, then falls.

This is why the motor can behave differently when cold and after prolonged operation, even with the same supply voltage and Power Limit setting. My calculator accounts for this when calculating minimum supply voltage and power.

5. Calculate the equivalent speed constant — the Kt/Kv relationship

Two useful constants describe a BLDC/PMSM motor: Kt, the torque constant, and Kv, the speed constant. They describe the same electrical machine from different perspectives.

5.1. What does Kt mean?

Kt specifies torque per unit of torque-producing current. For my motor:

Kt = 0.56 Nm/A
Kt
Torque constant in the current-amplitude convention [Nm/A]

In simplified terms, each ampere of Iq produces approximately 0.56 Nm. A larger Kt gives more torque at the same current.

5.2. What does Kv mean?

Kv describes the relationship between motor speed and an appropriately defined voltage. With sufficient voltage, a larger Kv allows higher speed at the same reference voltage.

A higher Kv is not always better. A motor designed to achieve high speed at low voltage generally requires more current for the same torque.

5.3. Why are Kt and Kv related?

In a permanent-magnet motor, the same electromagnetic construction produces torque from current and generates voltage when the rotor turns. The torque constant and voltage constant are therefore closely related.

With consistent SI units and appropriately matched voltage and current definitions:

Kt = Ke

Ke

6. Calculate the voltage needed for the required torque and speed

This is a key calculation. A motor may produce high torque at low speed but fail to maintain it at higher speed because of back-EMF, or back electromotive force.

6.1. What is back-EMF?

A BLDC/PMSM motor has a permanent-magnet rotor and a wound stator. Controlled winding current produces a magnetic field that interacts with the rotor magnets to generate torque.

Rotor rotation also has another effect. As magnets move relative to the stator windings, their linked magnetic flux changes. Faraday’s law of induction states that changing magnetic flux induces voltage. The rotating motor therefore generates voltage in its own windings.

This happens whether the rotor is turned by the controller or by an external force. During electrically driven operation, this induced voltage opposes the voltage used by the controller to establish current, hence the name back electromotive force.

6.2. Why does back-EMF rise with speed?

Faster rotor rotation changes the linked flux more quickly, increasing the generated voltage. The simplified relationship is linear:

E = Ke · ω
E
Voltage generated by rotor motion (back-EMF) [V]
Ke
Back-EMF voltage constant in the matched SI voltage/current convention [V/(rad/s)]
ω
Angular speed [rad/s]

E is generated voltage, Ke is the motor voltage constant, and ω is angular speed. At zero speed, ω = 0 and E = 0. At higher speed, back-EMF rises in proportion to rotational speed.

6.3. Why does back-EMF limit torque?

Torque primarily depends on current. Maintaining torque means maintaining winding current, which requires voltage to overcome both winding resistance and back-EMF.

Ureq = UEMF + UR
Ureq
Required voltage in the motor-model convention [V]
UEMF
Back-EMF voltage term in the model convention [V]
UR
Resistive voltage-drop term in the stated voltage convention [V]

UEMF is the back-EMF-related voltage term; UR is the resistive voltage term. At low speed, back-EMF is small and the controller has ample voltage headroom for current regulation. At high speed, rising back-EMF consumes more of the available voltage budget.

Once the controller reaches its voltage limit, it cannot maintain the commanded current and available torque falls. This is why supply voltage matters so much for maintaining torque at high speed. A large supply current rating alone is insufficient: the controller also needs enough voltage to establish the required current.

7. Account for PWM limits and select the supply voltage

The 20.02 V and 21.61 V calculated above are required voltages in the motor model convention. They are not yet supply nameplate voltages. To size the supply, I need to consider how the controller generates winding voltages.

7.1. How does a FOC controller power the motor?

The MKS XDrive Mini uses a three-phase MOSFET power bridge. Each phase is driven by a pair of transistors: the high-side transistor connects the phase node to the positive supply rail, and the low-side transistor connects it to the negative rail.

The controller does not directly generate an ideal analog sine-wave voltage. Instead, it rapidly switches the transistors using pulse-width modulation (PWM). By changing their on-times, it adjusts average winding voltage. Coordinated switching of all three phases creates the rotating voltage vector needed to regulate current and torque.

7.2. What is SVPWM?

Space Vector Pulse Width Modulation combines switching states of the three bridge legs. Within each short PWM period, it selects the state durations required to produce the desired average voltage vector. This uses the DC bus voltage efficiently to control the three-phase motor.

Modulation capability is finite. Eventually, the requested vector becomes too large to reproduce with the chosen modulation method and measurement requirements, and the controller reaches its voltage limit.

7.3. Why not use the full 100% of DC voltage?

Bridge hardware and timing impose practical limits. MOSFETs require time to turn on and off. A short dead time separates switching of the high-side and low-side transistors in the same leg, preventing simultaneous conduction that could short the supply rails.

Gate-signal propagation delays and current-measurement constraints also matter. My controller measures phase currents using two shunt resistors.

7.4. How do current shunts work?

A shunt is a very low-resistance resistor that develops a small voltage drop when current flows through it:

Ushunt = I · Rshunt
Ushunt
Measured voltage drop across a current-sense shunt [V]
I
Current through the specified conductor (RMS for sinusoidal heating calculations) [A]
Rshunt
Current-sense shunt resistance [Ω]

The measurement circuit amplifies that signal, and the microcontroller ADC converts it to a digital value. The controller can then calculate actual current and compare it with the FOC current command.

With two shunts, measuring two branch currents is sufficient because the third can be reconstructed from:

IA + IB + IC = 0
IA
Instantaneous phase A current [A]
IB
Instantaneous phase B current [A]
IC
Instantaneous phase C current [A]

7.5. Why can current sensing limit modulation?

For shunts in the low-side bridge legs, reliable current measurements are available only in suitable switching states. In particular, current through a conducting low-side transistor can pass through that leg’s shunt. The controller must provide an adequate measurement window.

Immediately after switching, noise and ringing can disturb the signal. It needs time to settle, followed by the ADC sampling time. At very high PWM duty cycles, low-side conduction may become too short for reliable measurement, making phase-current reconstruction difficult.

This matters especially for one- or two-shunt low-side sensing. Practical modulation range can therefore also be limited by current-sampling requirements. However, not every two-shunt design has the same limit: measurement electronics, PWM frequency, sampling strategy and control algorithm all affect it.

8. Calculate mechanical power

With the minimum voltage established and a supply voltage selected, I can calculate power. First, I need to explain the sources of the motor’s energy demand.

8.1. What makes up the power draw?

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy, but the process is not ideal. Some energy becomes mechanical work and the rest is lost, mainly as heat. My model separates four terms:

  • Mechanical power, Pmech: power delivered to the shaft, determined by torque and speed.
  • Copper losses, PCu: heat generated by current flowing through winding resistance.
  • Controller losses, Pdriver: assumed power-stage losses, including MOSFET conduction and switching.
  • Electronics draw, Pelectronics: power needed by the microcontroller, sensing circuits and other controller electronics.

Important model limitation

I size power for a defined scenario: 10 Nm at 200 rpm and at least 2 Nm at 300 rpm. “At least 2 Nm” does not mean the controller cannot produce more torque at 300 rpm.

For example, at 36 V the model also allows 10 Nm at 300 rpm. If the controller commands the full 10 Nm in the direction of rotation there, demand rises to approximately 441 W before the margin, or approximately 529 W including 20%.

The 397 W result is sizing for the defined operating points. It does not guarantee that the supply will never see a greater load.

To guarantee operation at any available torque throughout the speed range, I would need to analyze the full torque–speed characteristic.

During braking, when motor torque opposes rotation, energy can flow back toward the DC bus. That requires a separate regeneration analysis and must not be confused with positive supply power draw.

The principle behind the calculator

First, determine the voltage needed to maintain the required torque at the required speed. Then calculate the power needed for mechanical work and electrical losses.

This avoids sizing the supply solely from maximum motor current or no-load speed. It accounts for the practical requirements of a Direct Drive wheel: high torque during fast hand movements, plus adequate speed and torque during self-rotation.

The result is a starting point for informed supply selection, with explicit assumptions, equations and limits of the simplified model.
